# Principles for swapping exercises without losing training effect

Exercise substitution is sometimes necessary due to equipment availability, pain on a
specific movement, or a plateau — but a swap should preserve the training intent of the
slot it replaces.

Key points:

- Prefer swaps that keep the same primary movement pattern and muscle group emphasis (e.g.
  a horizontal push for a horizontal push) over swaps that change the movement pattern
  entirely, unless the reason for the swap is pain on that pattern specifically.
- When a swap is prompted by pain or a technique issue on the original exercise, the
  replacement should not simply be a harder or higher-skill variation; a more forgiving
  variation (e.g. supported/machine-based, reduced range of motion) is usually preferable
  until the underlying issue is understood.
- Load and rep targets on a new exercise should not be assumed to transfer 1:1 from the
  replaced exercise; the plan should generally hold or conservatively estimate load on a
  swapped exercise rather than projecting the old exercise's progression forward.
- A swap changes only that session or that plan slot by default — it should not silently
  rewrite the athlete's underlying template without an explicit, separate confirmation.
