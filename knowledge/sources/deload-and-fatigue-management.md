# Deloading and fatigue management

Accumulated training fatigue can outpace the rate of physiological adaptation, leading to
stalled progress, elevated injury risk, and degraded recovery markers.

Key points:

- A deload (temporary reduction in volume and/or intensity) is a proactive tool, not a
  punishment: it lets fatigue dissipate while largely preserving the adaptations already made.
- Signals that commonly justify reducing volume rather than continuing to add load include:
  repeated recovery-marker disruption (poor sleep, elevated resting heart rate), and stalled
  or regressing performance despite consistent effort.
- A single off day (e.g. one night of poor sleep) is normal and usually does not warrant a
  training change; a pattern across several sessions is a stronger signal.
- Reducing set count by one or two sets on affected exercises is a lighter-touch intervention
  than reducing load, and is often preferable when the athlete is still recovering technique
  and confidence at the current weight.
- Fatigue management decisions should always remain proposals for the athlete to confirm —
  automated systems should not silently rewrite a training plan.
